LET TEENAGERS SLEEP

I   Doctors agree that teenagers need about nine hours of sleep every night, but very few of them actually get it. During the teenage years, the body's inner clock moves forward, so most teenagers do not feel sleepy before eleven o'clock at night. Yet in many countries the school day begins at eight in the morning or even earlier, and millions of students sit through their first lesson feeling drowsy1. This lack of sleep is harmful because it weakens the memory and makes it harder to concentrate in lessons. Sleep researchers now call teenage tiredness a hidden health problem.

II   One possible answer sounds almost too simple: begin the school day later. Over the past few years, schools in the United States, Britain, and New Zealand have moved their first lesson from eight o'clock to nine. Because a later start matches the teenage body clock, students can get a full night's sleep without going to bed earlier, and they arrive at school more awake. "A teenager who begins lessons at nine learns much more than the same teenager at eight," says sleep researcher Karen Mills. "The first schools that tried it saw the difference within weeks."

III   However, later start times have not spread as fast as many expected. Changing the timetable2 affects the whole community: school buses must run at different hours, afternoon activities finish later, and many parents worry because they cannot drive their children to school before work. Attitude is a difficulty as well. "Many adults still believe that waking up early builds character," Mills explains. "They think a tired teenager is simply a lazy teenager, so they see no reason to change anything."

IV   Can these opinions change? A study carried out in Australia suggests that they can. Researchers asked a high school near Sydney to begin its lessons at nine o'clock instead of eight for one full term. During the experiment, the students kept sleep diaries3, writing down when they went to bed, when they woke up, and how tired they felt in class. To check whether the later start helped, the researchers also compared the students' grades with their grades from the term before.

V   The results were impressive. By the end of the term, the students were sleeping almost an hour more each night, and their grades improved in nearly every subject. "The extra sleep changed the mood of the whole school," says Mills. "Students who used to sit in class half asleep now take part in every lesson. My dream is that one day, every high school will give its students the gift of a later morning."
