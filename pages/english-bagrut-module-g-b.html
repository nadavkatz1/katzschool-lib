PART I: WRITTEN RECEPTION (60 points)
(ACCESS TO INFORMATION FROM WRITTEN TEXTS)

Read the article below and then answer questions 1-9.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE GOES TO SCHOOL
IWhen people talk about artificial intelligence (AI), they usually imagine self-driving cars,
robots working in factories, or computer programs that beat champions at chess. However, AI has
also quietly entered a place much closer to home: the classroom. AI learning programs are
computer systems that adjust themselves to each student, offering easier or harder exercises
5according to the student's answers. Millions of students around the world already use such
programs every week, and experts believe their number will grow dramatically over the next
decade.
IIIn recent years, a growing number of countries have begun bringing AI tools into their
schools. Estonia, a small country in northern Europe, is widely considered a leader in this
10field. Estonian schools began using digital learning materials years before most other
countries, and today many of their classrooms use AI programs that follow each pupil's
progress and report it to the teacher. The results attract attention worldwide: in
international tests, Estonian students are regularly ranked among the best in Europe, and
education ministers from many countries travel to Estonia to see its schools in action.
15IIIThere are good reasons for this interest. Research shows that students learn best when
they receive help at the exact moment they need it, and this is precisely what AI programs
are designed to do. "A teacher standing in front of thirty students cannot possibly check
every exercise immediately," explains Dr. Laura Bennett, an education researcher at a
Toronto university. "An AI program checks each answer within seconds, explains the mistake
20and offers another exercise at the right level. For a struggling student, that immediate
response can make all the difference." According to Bennett, teachers who use such programs
also gain hours of extra time every week, which they can spend working with the students
who need them most.
IVHowever, AI in the classroom also raises serious concerns. To adjust themselves to each
25student, AI programs must collect enormous amounts of information about the children who
use them: every answer, every hesitation and every mistake is recorded and stored. Parents'
organizations warn that schools rarely know where this information is kept or who can see
it. Cost is another problem. The most advanced programs are expensive, so wealthy schools
can afford far better tools than schools in poorer areas. Instead of closing gaps between
30students, critics warn, AI could actually make them wider.
VTeachers have noticed a further difficulty: some students let the technology do their
thinking for them. Chatbots can now write essays, solve math problems and summarize whole
books within seconds, and teachers around the world report receiving homework that was
clearly produced by a machine. "The danger is not that students use AI, but that they stop
35making an effort," says Bennett. "Learning happens when your brain struggles with a
problem. If a machine does the struggling for you, the grade may look fine, but very
little has actually been learned."
VIGovernments have recently begun responding to these concerns. New European guidelines
require schools to check how learning programs protect students' information, and several
40countries have published rules about when chatbots may be used for homework. Critics say
the rules will be difficult to enforce, but supporters insist they are an essential first
step. Bennett remains optimistic. "No program will ever replace a good teacher," she says.
"But if teachers stay in charge and use AI wisely, it can give every child the personal
attention that crowded classrooms could never provide. Used this way, AI will not weaken
45education. It will strengthen it."

PART II: WRITTEN PRODUCTION (40 points)
(WRITTEN PRESENTATION)

Write 120-140 words in English on the following topic.

10.
Some schools around the world have moved to a four-day school week, with longer school days and an extra day off. Do you think this is a good idea?
Write a composition stating and explaining your opinion. Give reasons and examples to support your answer.
(40 points)

Write your final version here:

MODEL COMPOSITION (132 words) The route · הדרך: The task is an opinion composition worth 40 points: state what you think about the four-day school week and support it, rather than survey both sides evenly. Plan before writing a word - an opening sentence that says your opinion plainly, two reasons in the body with an example under each, one sentence that answers the opposing view, and a conclusion that returns to the opinion in different words. The register is formal but personal, so 'In my opinion' and 'I believe' belong here while slang and contractions do not, and the parts are joined with Firstly, Secondly, However and In conclusion. The model answer below keeps the present simple for the opinion and for what is true today, and moves to the second conditional ('If school continued until the evening, students would come home too tired') for the results of a change that has not happened. Count the words: 120-140 is a requirement, and spelling mistakes cost points.
מטלת דעה של 40 נקודות. בוחרים צד, פותחים במשפט עמדה ברור, שני נימוקים ולכל אחד דוגמה, התייחסות אחת לדעה הנגדית וסיום שחוזר לעמדה. הסגנון רשמי אך אישי, ומילות הקישור Firstly, Secondly, However ו-In conclusion מחברות בין החלקים. התשובה לדוגמה כתובה בהווה פשוט, ועוברת לתנאי מסוג שני כשהיא מתארת מה היה קורה אילו הלימודים היו נמשכים עד הערב. 120-140 מילים, ועל שגיאות כתיב מורידים נקודות.

In my opinion, schools should not move to a four-day week. Firstly, longer school days are exhausting. Most teenagers already find it hard to concentrate during the last lessons of the day. If school continued until the evening, students would come home too tired to do homework or enjoy their hobbies. Secondly, an extra day off would not help every family. Many parents work five days a week, so younger children would stay home alone, and some families would have to pay for babysitters. Supporters argue that a long weekend gives students time to rest. However, I believe that many teenagers would simply spend the extra day on their phones. In conclusion, although a four-day week sounds attractive, I am convinced that five shorter days are better for students and their families.

Why this composition earns full marks: it states a clear opinion in the introduction, gives two reasons, each supported by an example, answers an opposing argument, uses linking words (Firstly, Secondly, However, In conclusion) and restates the opinion at the end - all within the required 120-140 words.

How the composition is assessed: content and organization (a clear, well-supported opinion), vocabulary, and language accuracy. Remember: points are deducted for incorrect spelling.
